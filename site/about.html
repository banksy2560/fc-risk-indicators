About FC Risk Indicators

An open catalogue of financial crime risk indicators (red flags) organised by typology, each paired with candidate controls. It covers money laundering, terrorist and proliferation financing, sanctions evasion, fraud, bribery and corruption, tax evasion, human trafficking, environmental crime, cyber-enabled crime and cross-cutting factors.

Status

  • Content was drafted with AI assistance and has not been validated by subject-matter experts.
  • Source citations were written from model knowledge. No citation has been confirmed by a human: check any citation before relying on it.
  • Indicators are red flags, not proof of crime. Nothing here is legal or compliance advice, and no institution should treat it as a complete or approved risk assessment.
  • Right now the library holds 115 typologies, 676 canonical indicators and 177 canonical controls across 19 control families.

How linking works

Every indicator and every control is defined once and linked many times. Nothing is copied between typologies.

TypologyThe unit of analysis. Owns a description, sectors and sources.
links to
IndicatorAn observable red flag, defined once. Context and false-positive notes can be typology-specific.
mitigated by
ControlA candidate control, defined once, reused across many indicators and typologies.
  • A typology links to canonical indicators. Each link states how the indicator shows up in that typology and names the controls used there.
  • A control page lists every indicator and typology that uses it; an indicator page lists its typologies and controls. "Used in N typologies" simply counts these links.
  • Adding something that already exists in spirit means linking the existing item, not creating a near-duplicate.
  • Search finds any item; Typologies gives a citable page for each; Coverage shows link counts per control family.

Design principles

This library is deliberately not a risk register, an RCSA or a competing risk-assessment methodology. It is a reusable input that an institution's own ML/TF/fraud risk assessment consumes.

Typology is the unit

Products, channels, payment rails and customer segments are exposure dimensions, not separate registers. A typology is owned once, not duplicated across product views.

No scoring

The library prescribes no residual-risk calculation and no worst-of aggregation. Signal (high, medium, low) is indicative strength of an indicator, never a risk rating and never rolled up.

Input, not methodology

It supplies what is often missing: a shared, citable catalogue of observable indicators and candidate controls. Scoring and prioritisation stay with the adopting institution.

No institution data

It contains candidate controls and no assessments of any institution. Gap assessments belong in the adopter's own environment and governance.

Human review before trust

Every item is draft until a named, independent subject-matter expert reviews it. AI-drafted content is labelled as such.

Versioned, not live

Only versioned releases and review dates are promised. There is no continuous-update claim, and detection bias means you should not calibrate against your own SAR or loss data alone.

Counts and colours on the Coverage page describe linkage only. They are not effectiveness, adequacy or risk.

Citation status tags

Each typology source carries a tag. No citation has been confirmed by a human.

  • agent-reviewed an AI agent searched for it and found a matching real document (a listed URL may not have been fetched). Still verify before relying on it.
  • corrected, agent-reviewed the original title, year or publisher was wrong and was corrected by the same process.
  • unverified and corrected, unverified not confirmed by search; from model knowledge.
  • too vague, unverified names a body or topic but not a specific document.
  • replacement suggested, unverified the original could not be found; a nearest real document is suggested.
  • corrected, checked corrected and checked by search; still not human-confirmed.

Glossary

AML/CTF
Anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing.
AML/CTF program
The documented set of policies, procedures and controls a reporting entity maintains to identify, mitigate and manage money laundering and terrorism financing risk.
Reporting entity
A business or person with AML/CTF obligations, such as a bank, payments firm, crypto exchange, casino or other regulated service provider. The exact term varies by jurisdiction.
SMR / SAR / STR
Suspicious matter report, suspicious activity report or suspicious transaction report: a report a reporting entity files with the financial intelligence unit when it suspects crime. Terminology differs by jurisdiction.
Predicate offence
The underlying criminal offence (for example fraud, bribery or drug trafficking) that generates the proceeds being laundered.
Typology
A recognisable method or scheme by which a financial crime is carried out. In this library, the unit of analysis.
Indicator (red flag)
An observable pattern or characteristic that may point to a typology. It is not proof of crime and often has legitimate explanations.
Control
A measure that prevents, detects, corrects or governs a risk, such as a monitoring rule, a verification step or a training programme. Here, controls are candidates, not assessments of what any institution has.
Control family
A grouping of controls by purpose, such as customer due diligence or transaction monitoring.
Signal
The indicative strength of an indicator (high, medium, low). Not a risk rating and never aggregated into a score.
Typical effectiveness
An indicative, drafted label on a control. It is not a measurement and not an assessment of any institution.
CDD / KYC
Customer due diligence and know your customer: identifying and verifying customers and understanding their activity.
EDD
Enhanced due diligence: additional scrutiny applied to higher-risk customers or situations.
PEP
Politically exposed person: someone with a prominent public function, and often their family and close associates, who may present higher bribery and corruption risk.
UBO / beneficial owner
The natural person who ultimately owns or controls a customer or legal entity.
TBML
Trade-based money laundering: disguising proceeds through trade transactions, such as over- or under-invoicing.
MVTS
Money or value transfer service.
DNFBP
Designated non-financial businesses and professions, such as dealers, lawyers, accountants and corporate service providers.
FATF
Financial Action Task Force, the intergovernmental body that sets international AML/CTF standards.
RCSA
Risk and control self-assessment, a common internal method this library deliberately does not replace.
Residual risk
Risk remaining after controls are considered. This library does not calculate it.
False positive
A flagged case that turns out to have a legitimate explanation. Each indicator lists common ones.

Contribute or review

Source, data and issue tracker are on GitHub: github.com/banksy2560/fc-risk-indicators.

  • Review. SME review, corrections and verified citations are the most valuable contributions. Mark reviewed items status: sme_reviewed with last_reviewed. Reviewers should be independent of whoever drafted the content.
  • Extend. Propose new typologies, indicators or controls. Search first, and link an existing item rather than adding a near-duplicate.
  • Check merges. Borderline AI merges are listed in the repository's review queue; each typology link keeps its legacy id so a merge can be split out.
  • Do not submit real customer, case or personal data.
  • The site is rebuilt from the YAML files by tools/build_site.py.

Known gaps

Drawn from the repository's review queue. Treat the catalogue as incomplete.

  • Merges awaiting review. Indicators and controls were de-duplicated by AI clustering. Two controls, "Customer enquiry" and "Profile-versus-activity deviation", are broad merges; "Typology-based scenario design", "Product-specific misuse surveillance" and "Sector-tailored EDD" may need splitting.
  • Missing controls. No suitable catalogue control yet for: independent AML programme-effectiveness testing; alert-queue ageing and capacity forecasting; order-book and spoofing surveillance; licensing and change-in-control fit-and-proper review; court-record verification; safe-deposit-box access logging; transfer-pricing benchmarking; cross-border real-time freeze or recall coordination; scam-compound infrastructure or telecoms-spend monitoring; a unified fraud and AML view across silos. Links to the "closest fit" are approximate.
  • Thin governance coverage. Institutional-governance indicators lean on one sampling-QA control as the only detective option. The family corrective response has a single control.
  • Data quality. Some singleton indicators keep original typology-flavoured wording and generic default detection types, data sources and false-positive notes.
  • Not yet populated. Exposure tags (channels, payment rails, customer segments, products), evidence basis, confidence, provenance and review fields.
  • Citations. All unverified or agent-checked only, as described above.
